The dashboard is the first screen people see after they log in. A **dashboard widget** puts something of your
plugin's there: a summary, a reminder, a shortcut. Add one only when people will want it every time they log in.
Everything else belongs on your plugin's own screen, as in
[Adding a settings page](/guides/path/adding-a-settings-page/).

## Adding a widget

Add widgets on the <Sym id="action:wp_dashboard_setup" /> action, which fires as the dashboard screen loads.
<Sym id="function:wp_add_dashboard_widget" /> takes an id, a title, and a callback that prints the widget's content:

<Example id="guides/adding-a-dashboard-widget/add-a-widget" />

The callback prints, so it escapes what it prints and translates its strings, like any other output. Keep it quick:
it runs on every visit to the dashboard. Something slow to work out, such as a count over many rows, belongs in a
transient that a scheduled task refreshes, as
[Running tasks on a schedule](/guides/path/running-tasks-on-a-schedule/) shows.

## Who sees it, and where

Check the capability before adding the widget, so people who can't use it never see it. The dashboard lays widgets
out in columns, and `wp_add_dashboard_widget()` takes a **context**, `normal` or `side`, and a **priority**, `high`,
`core`, `default` or `low`, for where it starts. People can drag widgets elsewhere and hide them under Screen
Options, and WordPress remembers their choice.

A plugin can also take widgets away. <Sym id="function:remove_meta_box" /> removes one by its id, its screen and its
context. Do it on the same action with a later priority, after WordPress has added its own:

<Example id="guides/adding-a-dashboard-widget/who-sees-it-and-where" />

The administrator got the widget and the subscriber didn't, and WordPress Events and News was removed. Think twice
before removing widgets people may rely on. Hiding them under Screen Options is each person's choice to make.

## A settings form

Pass a fourth argument, a **control callback**, and the widget gets a Configure link that shows a form in its place.
The control callback both prints the form and saves it when it is posted:

<Example id="guides/adding-a-dashboard-widget/configure-a-widget" />

WordPress adds the form's nonce field itself, and checks it with <Sym id="function:check_admin_referer" /> before it
calls your control callback, then sends the browser back to the dashboard. The example calls the control directly,
to show the save. Your callback still checks the capability before it saves, because a nonce proves where the request
came from, not that the person may change the setting. It sanitizes the value, as
[Keeping your plugin safe](/guides/path/keeping-your-plugin-safe/) explains.

The admin screens aren't ported to SpeedySpec yet, so this guide makes no promise about how the dashboard behaves
there.
