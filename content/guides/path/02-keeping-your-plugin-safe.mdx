---
title: Keeping your plugin safe
summary: Validate what comes in, sanitize it, escape what goes out, and check nonces and capabilities before acting.
---

Your plugin's code runs with the whole site's power: it can read every option, change every post and act as whoever
is logged in. Whatever arrives with a request (a form field, a query string, a cookie, a value another site sent
back) was written by someone you don't know. Most plugin vulnerabilities come down to trusting one of those values
too early. This guide gives you the habits that stop that, each with a checked example.

The habits fit together like this:

- **On the way in:** validate the value, and refuse it if it isn't what you expect. Sanitize what you keep. Check a
  nonce and a capability before acting on the request.
- **On the way out:** escape every value, for the place you print it.

## Validate what you expect

Validating asks a yes-or-no question: is this an email address, a number in range, one of the choices you offered?
When the answer is no, refuse the value and say why. Don't quietly turn it into something else, because then the
person never learns their input was wrong.

## Sanitize on the way in

Sanitizing cleans a value so it is safe to store: it drops the parts that don't belong. WordPress has one sanitizer
per kind of value, and the right choice depends on what the field should hold.

<Example id="guides/keeping-your-plugin-safe/sanitize-by-kind" />

- <Sym id="function:sanitize_text_field" /> keeps one line of plain text. Tags are removed, a script's content
  with them, and so are line breaks and surrounding space.
- <Sym id="function:sanitize_email" /> keeps only the characters an address may have. It does not check that what is
  left is an address: that is `is_email()`'s job.
- <Sym id="function:sanitize_key" /> makes a lowercase identifier, for slugs and keys.
- <Sym id="function:absint" /> makes a whole number of zero or more. Notice that `-12 apples` became `12`: sanitizing
  never tells you the input was nonsense. Validate first when a wrong value matters.

Values from `$_POST` and `$_GET` arrive with slashes added, so pass them through <Sym id="function:wp_unslash" />
before sanitizing. <OnSpeedySpecNote id="function:sanitize_text_field" />

## Escape on the way out

Escaping makes a value harmless for the exact place it is printed, so the browser reads it as text and never as
markup or code. The rule is **escape late**: at the moment of output, every time, even for values you sanitized when
you saved them. A value in the database may have been written by another plugin, an import or an older version of
your own code.

<Example id="guides/keeping-your-plugin-safe/escape-by-context" />

Each place has its own function:

- <Sym id="function:esc_html" /> for text between tags.
- <Sym id="function:esc_attr" /> for an attribute's value, always inside quotes.
- <Sym id="function:esc_url" /> for a link or a `src`. It also refuses protocols such as `javascript:`.
- <Sym id="function:wp_kses_post" /> for content that may hold the tags a post may hold, and nothing else.

## Nonces: was this request meant?

Imagine a page elsewhere on the web that quietly submits a form to your plugin's settings screen. If a logged-in
administrator visits it, their browser sends their cookies along, and without a check your plugin would obey. A
**nonce** stops that. It is a token your own form includes, made by <Sym id="function:wp_create_nonce" /> for one
action and the current user, and it is valid for a limited time (about a day by default). The handler checks it with
<Sym id="function:wp_verify_nonce" />.

<Example id="guides/keeping-your-plugin-safe/nonce-checks" />

Name the action as precisely as the request: `my_plugin_delete_item_7`, not just `delete`, so a nonce for one item
can't be replayed against another. In a form, <Sym id="function:wp_nonce_field" /> prints the hidden field, and on
an admin screen <Sym id="function:check_admin_referer" /> verifies it and stops the request when it fails.

A nonce proves that a request came from your form. It doesn't prove the person may do what they asked: that takes a
capability check.

## Capabilities: may this person do it?

Every logged-in person has a role, and every role grants **capabilities**, such as `manage_options` for site
settings or `edit_posts` for writing. Before your plugin acts for someone, ask whether they have the capability the
action needs, with <Sym id="function:current_user_can" />. Check a capability, never a role name: sites change what
roles may do, and capability checks follow those changes.

<Example id="guides/keeping-your-plugin-safe/capability-checks" />

Check on every request that changes something, including the request that saves a form, not only on the page that
shows the form. Hiding a button is not a permission check. <OnSpeedySpecNote id="function:current_user_can" />

## Redirect only where you meant to

After saving, plugins often send the browser somewhere, sometimes to a URL the request named. An unchecked target

## Putting it together

A request handler applies the habits in order: the nonce, then the capability, then validation and sanitizing, and
only then the change.

The first request has no nonce, and the second has an address that isn't one. The third is saved, with its surrounding
spaces gone. The last comes from a subscriber with their own valid nonce, and the capability check refuses it.

None of the seams used here is ported to SpeedySpec yet, so this guide makes no promise about how these functions
behave there.
