---
title: Users, roles and capabilities
summary: Check capabilities rather than roles, add roles and capabilities on activation, remove them on uninstall, and store per-person settings.
---

Everyone who logs in to a WordPress site is a **user**, and every user has a **role**: Administrator, Editor, Author,
Contributor or Subscriber, plus any a plugin adds. A role is only a named bundle of **capabilities**, the individual
things a person may do, such as `edit_posts`, `upload_files` or `manage_options`. This guide shows how your plugin
asks what a person may do, how it adds abilities of its own, and how it remembers settings per person.

## Check capabilities, not roles

When your plugin decides whether someone may do something, ask about the capability the action needs. Never ask
which role they have. A site's owner can change what each role may do, with a role editor plugin or a line of code,
and capability checks follow those changes automatically. A role check doesn't:

<Example id="guides/users-roles-and-capabilities/check-capabilities-not-roles" />

The editor was allowed to manage books, and the capability check knew it. The role check refused them, because they
aren't an administrator. <Sym id="function:current_user_can" /> asks about the person logged in now, and
<Sym id="function:user_can" /> about any user.

Some capabilities need to know which thing is meant. `edit_post`, `delete_post` and `edit_user` take an ID:
`current_user_can( 'edit_post', $post_id )`. WordPress turns these into the real checks for that object (whether it
is theirs, whether it is published) through <Sym id="function:map_meta_cap" />. Use the specific form whenever you
act on one item. <OnSpeedySpecNote id="function:current_user_can" />

## Your plugin's own capabilities

When your plugin adds something people may or may not be allowed to do, give it a capability of its own, prefixed
like everything else: `my_plugin_manage_books`. Then site owners decide who gets it, and your code checks only that
one name.

Roles and their capabilities are saved in the database, not rebuilt on each request like post types. So add them
once, in your activation hook, with <Sym id="function:add_role" /> for a new role, and the `add_cap()` method of
<Sym id="class:WP_Role" /> for an existing one:

<Example id="guides/users-roles-and-capabilities/add-role-on-activation" />

Give administrators your capabilities too, or the people running the site can't use the feature. `add_role()` returns
`null` when the role already exists and leaves it unchanged, so reactivating the plugin is safe. It won't update an
existing role's capabilities, though. To change them in a later version, use `add_cap()` and `remove_cap()` on the
role, from an upgrade routine.

## Removing them on uninstall

Because roles live in the database, they outlast your plugin. Remove them in `uninstall.php` with
<Sym id="function:remove_role" />, and take your capabilities back from the roles you gave them to. Removing a role
strips its people of every capability, even `read`, so move them to a basic role first:

<Example id="guides/users-roles-and-capabilities/remove-role-on-uninstall" />

Lena kept her account and can still read, as a subscriber. Do this on uninstall, not on deactivation, for the same
reason as settings: people deactivate a plugin to test something and expect their setup to survive.

## Settings per person

A setting that belongs to one person, such as their reading goal or a preference in your plugin's screen, is
**user meta**, stored with their account. Register the key with <Sym id="function:register_meta" />, using the
`user` object type, a type, a sanitize callback and an auth callback, the same way as post meta:

<Example id="guides/users-roles-and-capabilities/user-meta" />

<Sym id="function:update_user_meta" /> ran the sanitizer, so `24 books` was saved as `24`. Look at what
<Sym id="function:get_user_meta" /> returned, though: the string `'24'`. Meta is stored as text, so cast numbers when
you read them. The registered default only comes back as the integer `0` because Sam has no value saved. The auth
callback uses the meta capability `edit_user`, so a person may change their own goal, and administrators anyone's.

## Logged-out visitors

Most visitors to a site aren't logged in. To WordPress they are user `0`, with no roles and no capabilities. Test for
that with <Sym id="function:is_user_logged_in" /> before using the current user's details:

<Example id="guides/users-roles-and-capabilities/logged-out-visitor" />

<Sym id="function:wp_get_current_user" /> always returns a user object, even for a visitor, so code that reads its
name without checking prints an empty name instead of failing. A capability check is always false for a visitor,
which is why the capability checks from [Keeping your plugin safe](/guides/path/keeping-your-plugin-safe/) also turn
away anyone who isn't logged in.

The Identity seam, which holds users, roles and capabilities, isn't ported to SpeedySpec yet, so this guide makes no
promise about how they behave there.
